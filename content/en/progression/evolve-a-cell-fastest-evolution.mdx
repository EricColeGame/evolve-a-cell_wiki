## Reading the pond before you commit

Every run starts with the same problem: you are small, slow relative to bigger things, and edible. The advertised design gives you two escape routes from that position, and understanding them is most of the early game.

Grazing on plants is the lower-risk path. It does not require you to win a fight, and it keeps you moving through the pond rather than parking in one contested spot. Predation is the faster, louder path: hunting other cells, including players, can reward aggression, but it also puts you in the same water as creatures that may be better built for combat than you are. The official page does not say which path yields more growth, and no verified source publishes growth values, so treat any claim of a mathematically optimal route as unproven.

A few practical habits that follow from the advertised design, without inventing numbers:

- Watch other cells before approaching them. The description explicitly warns the pond is full of hungry things, and other players are listed as potential prey.
- Spend your first minutes learning what your current parts let you do. Jaws, spikes, eyes and tails are advertised as functional evolutions, which implies each changes your capabilities rather than just your look.
- Use the shape and paint tools early. Customization is a headline feature, and building a silhouette you can recognize at a distance helps you track your own cell in busy water.
- Do not assume a bigger cell is automatically a threat. Some large creatures may be grazing builds. The official page does not define threat behavior, so observation beats assumption.

## Evolving with intent, not guesswork

Beyond that list, the official description does not document mutation names, costs, requirements, or how parts interact. That gap matters, because it means nobody outside the development team can tell you with authority that a specific combination is strongest.

What you can do is reason from function. Jaws suggest offense or feeding. Spikes suggest defense or deterrence. Eyes suggest perception, which in a pond full of hidden threats is its own kind of survival tool. Tails suggest movement. These are reasonable inferences from the advertised parts, not confirmed mechanics, and the game may implement them in ways the description does not spell out.

If you want certainty, the honest answer is to test in-game: add a part, observe what changes about your cell's behavior and survivability, and adjust. Because the game supports up to 16 players per server, the pond's pressure changes with who else is present, so a build that works in a quiet server may struggle in a crowded one. That is a design consequence of multiplayer, not a documented rule.

## Getting to the boss stage

The official description promises that you can grow huge and take down giant bosses. It does not name the bosses, list their health, describe their attacks, or state what size you need to reach before fighting them. What the advertised arc does tell you is directional: growth precedes boss combat. Spending your early and mid-game building a cell that can both survive the pond and deal damage is the reasonable interpretation. Since predation and grazing are both advertised, a player who only grazes may reach boss content with a build untested against hostile creatures, and a player who only hunts may neglect the steady growth that plants provide. Balancing the two is a sensible approach, though the game does not publish ratios to follow.
